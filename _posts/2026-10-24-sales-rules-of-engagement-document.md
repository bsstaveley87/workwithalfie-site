## Why write rules of engagement down?

Because the rules already exist, just in someone's head. "Enterprise accounts go to Dana." "Don't move a deal to Proposal without a close date." Those work while the team is small enough to ask.

They stop working when the team grows. Two reps claim the same account, and nobody can say which rule wins. A new hire guesses. The HubSpot setup reflects whoever set it up first, not how the team works today. Writing the rules down turns arguments into lookups.

## What goes in a rules of engagement document?

Five sections cover most growing SaaS teams. Keep each rule to a sentence or two, and number them so people can point to them.

| Section | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| 1. Account and lead ownership | Who owns this record, and how was that decided? |
| 2. Disputes and exceptions | Two people claim it. Who wins, and who decides? |
| 3. Required data | What must this record have before it moves on? |
| 4. Duplicates and company hierarchy | Is this one company or two, and how do they link? |
| 5. Approvals and changes to the rules | Who has to say yes, and who can change the rules? |

### 1. Account and lead ownership

Who owns what, and how it's decided.

- How accounts are assigned: by territory, segment, company size, or round robin
- Whether there's a limit on how many named accounts one rep can hold
- What happens when a rep leaves or changes role
- How long an account stays with a rep without activity before it goes back to the pool

Example: "1.1 Named accounts are assigned by territory. Moving an account to a different rep needs approval from the sales lead."

### 2. Disputes and exceptions

What happens when two people claim the same account or lead.

- Who wins by default (for example, the existing account owner, or whoever logged the first meeting)
- Who decides when the default doesn't fit
- How long the decision should take

### 3. Required data

What each record needs, and when.

- Required fields at each deal stage
- What a contact needs before it can be added to a sequence
- How company names, domains and countries are written

Example: "2.3 A deal at Proposal needs a close date, amount, and primary contact. Deals that reach Proposal without them are flagged to the owner." ### 4. Duplicates and company hierarchy

How you keep one record per real-world thing.

- When two records count as duplicates (same domain, same email)
- Which values are kept in a merge
- How subsidiaries and regional offices link to a parent company, and who owns each

### 5. Approvals and changes to the rules

Who signs off on what.

- Which changes need a person to approve: moving an account to a different rep, merging important accounts, changing a closed deal
- Who that person is for each
- Who can change the rules themselves, and how changes are announced

## How long should it be?

Short enough that people read it. As a rule of thumb, two to four pages is plenty for a small team. If a rule needs a paragraph of explanation, it's probably two rules.

Write it in plain words, not legal language. Rules people understand get followed. Rules they don't get worked around.

## Where should the document live?

Where your team already works. A Notion page or a Google Doc is fine. What matters more:

- **One version.** Not a copy in every team's folder.
- **A named owner** who approves changes.
- **A change log** at the top: what changed, when and why.

## How do you get from a document to rules HubSpot follows?

A document on its own relies on people remembering it. The next step is to make the CRM check it. Some rules map to HubSpot settings, such as required properties on deal stages. Others, like who wins an ownership dispute, need a check before the change is made and a person to approve the exceptions.

## How do you keep it up to date?

Update the document whenever the process changes, not once a year. A new territory, a new product or a new hire is a prompt to check the rules. If the CRM is checking the rules, the document and the CRM have to change together, or they drift apart again.
